# Independent challenge: the default software-agent loop

Research date: 2026-11-23. Source assessed: [Default loop: thin orchestrator, strong planner, mid-tier builder](../../concepts/default-loop.md). Scope: challenge its starting assumption for nontrivial software work, not planning, delegation, or enforcement in general.

The source is explicitly tentative: it permits a single agent for small, clear tasks, treats roles as responsibilities rather than mandatory calls, calls for evidence before planning and replanning when assumptions break, and recommends a stronger builder when a clear assignment repeatedly proves hard. It does **not** prescribe a rigid upfront plan, a permanently weaker builder, or prompt-only enforcement. The question here is whether its initial allocation and the boundary between exceptions are the best starting point for nontrivial work.

## Bottom line

The source bundles testable choices about when to separate planning, where to spend model capacity, and how much judgment to retain at task boundaries. None is a general empirical law. In particular, a coding-agent compute-allocation study reports a useful **alternative allocation**: inexpensive planning workers supporting a strong lead that implements and verifies. Other evidence shows coordination can lose to a solo agent and plans can harm when mismatched to the task or model. The challenge is not that the source forbids these alternatives—it explicitly allows adaptation—but that waiting for repeated builder struggles, or classifying nontrivial work as needing distinct planning, may spend the budget too late or in the wrong place.

## Evidence and counterarguments

1. **The best use of a strong model may be implementation as well as planning.** Cohere's [Scaling Inference-Time Compute with Teams of Coding Agents](https://cohere.com/research/papers/scaling-inference-time-compute-with-teams-of-coding-agents-2026-10-12) varies delegation stage, model mix, and worker count. On ProgramBench, a strong lead with cheap planners recovered 47.5% of the all-strong team's reward gain for 13.9% of its additional cost; cheap implementers reduced reward. Delegating planning alone while the lead implemented and verified matched the all-stage delegation team's reward at half the cost. This challenges *starting* with a mid-tier builder, even though the source allows upgrading after repeated struggles. It does not show that a weak architect replaces a strong planner: the strong lead still makes decisions, and worker planning includes investigating a black-box program. The small Commit0-Lite comparison favored implementation delegation instead. These results are not an equal-cost role-allocation ablation.

2. **A solo loop is a meaningful baseline even on code tasks.** Kim et al., [Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v3), compare single-agent and four multi-agent architectures across 260 configurations and six benchmarks. On SWE-bench Verified, all tested multi-agent variants had lower mean performance than the single-agent baseline; the degradation varied by topology. On a sequential planning benchmark all multi-agent variants also lost, while centralized teams improved decomposable financial reasoning. This is evidence of task-architecture interaction, not a universal ban on delegation; the controlled architectures and models are not the exact proposed three-role stack. The paper's cross-validated predictive fit is modest and its empirical saturation threshold should not be treated as an operational cutoff.

3. **More agents can cost substantially more without a reliable quality gain.** Vals AI's [Do Agent Teams Pay Off?](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/multi-agent-vibe-code-bench) compares solo and native agent teams on app-building tasks for two models at two reasoning levels. Teams cost 1.8–5.1 times as much; only one of four team-versus-solo score differences was statistically significant. Increasing solo reasoning effort was another option, although in one reported comparison it cost more and took longer than the medium-effort team. This is a vendor-run benchmark, not proof of equivalence across software work; nonsignificance is not proof of no effect. It does show why raw quality, cost, and elapsed time must be compared together.

4. **A plan may be wrong, ignored, or mismatched to execution.** [Evaluating Plan Compliance in Autonomous Programming Agents](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.12147v2) analyzes SWE-agent trajectories across models and plan variants. Its standard plan and periodic reminders can improve resolution, but subpar plans can perform worse than no plan, and extra early phases can interfere with a model's workflow. The harder SWE-bench Pro analysis selected only 31 instances resolvable by at least one model under its standard plan, limiting generalization. The source already calls for evidence before planning and for replanning on broken assumptions; this study supports that caution rather than refuting it. Remaining uncertainty is whether the evidence and authority reach the decision-maker in time, and whether a handoff loses useful exploratory context.

5. **Coordination is real work, not just a thin routing function.** In [Decentralized Multi-Agent Coordination and Communication in Shared Codebases](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.00314), one agent given both specifications substantially outperformed parallel agents when specifications conflicted; one reported comparison was 62% versus 16%. Communication recovered only part of the gap and added tokens. The solo agent had both requirements from the outset, so it is an oracle relative to independently commissioned agents, not always a feasible production comparator. Still, it exposes a concrete failure mode: splitting coupled work can create interface conflicts and lose global context. In contrast, a [shared-workspace coordination study](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ESEM.2026.18) reports gains over solo agents when two agents receive peer-activity observations, illustrating that coordination design can help rather than always hurt.

6. **Strict enforcement needs external authority and evidence, not merely a strict prompt.** A planner–executor security benchmark, [PEAR](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-eacl.237/), finds substantial attack success in its tested threat models and reports that planner corruption can propagate into downstream execution. Its security tasks and attack assumptions are not a measurement of ordinary coding-agent reliability. The narrower inference is that a coordinator's instructions are not a security boundary: permissions, sandboxing, deterministic checks, review of diffs and tests, and explicit approval for sensitive actions must be enforced by the surrounding system where applicable. This is consistent with the source's enforcement requirement, not a finding that thin orchestration fails; the unresolved issue is whether the proposed runtime can identify and enforce the *right* checks without substantive judgment.

## Evidence that limits this challenge

The source links its own [supporting review](../thin-orchestration-smart-planners.md), which reports two particularly relevant fixed-worker comparisons. [PAUSE](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.29870v1) gives a stronger planner the worker's investigation transcript before the first edit; SWE-bench Verified resolution rose from 50.2% to 65.0% in one harness, with gains also reported in two others. [DCAS](https://doi.org/10.1145/3832783.3834485) reports 42.8% without an explicit plan, 48.2% with a self-generated plan, and 57.8% with a Sonnet 4.5 plan for the same executor and scaffold. These are concrete reasons to take a strong planner seriously, particularly when the builder's diagnosis is weak. Neither establishes a win against a capable solo agent at equal total cost: PAUSE matches executor budgets rather than total spend, and DCAS permits extra read-only planning turns. The source's [adaptive-leads review](../adaptive-model-leads.md) also documents cases where targeted intervention helped, without establishing that ongoing oversight is always worth its cost.

Thus the empirical dispute is about the **default and escalation timing**, not whether planner assistance ever helps. A bounded but nontrivial repair could justify solo-first, a pre-edit consultation, or strong-builder-first depending on local results; the available sources do not identify a universal boundary.

## Discriminating alternatives to test

These are hypotheses for local evaluation, not binding architecture choices:

* **Solo strong builder:** one agent explores, plans just enough, implements, and runs checks; compare against the full proposed stack, including its orchestration and handoff costs.
* **Strong builder plus inexpensive planning support:** a cheap agent proposes or critiques a short plan, while the strong builder owns implementation, replanning, and verification.
* **Adaptive escalation:** begin with one capable agent; add planning, independent review, or parallel work only for specific triggers such as unresolved ambiguity, independently testable modules, or high-risk changes. Test the trigger itself; do not assume the router reliably recognizes difficulty.
* **Risk-based verification:** enforce permissions and mandatory checks independently of the agent architecture. For consequential changes, compare independent review or adversarial testing against spending the same budget on better implementation or additional solo attempts.

Use the same representative tasks and repository snapshots; randomize configuration order, use repeated runs where feasible, and report task success and regressions under independent hidden tests or human review, total token/API cost, wall time, handoffs, and intervention burden. Compare both fixed-cost and fixed-time budgets. Include coupled debugging, cross-module work, and genuinely separable tasks; predeclare meaningful improvement thresholds. Track plan defects, handoff omissions, failed checks, and whether replanning was allowed. No cited result directly validates this exact model mix, current harness, or local workload.

To test the source fairly, give its arm the evidence-gathering and exception-triggered replanning it specifies, and let it upgrade a struggling builder. Separately measure the cost and failures incurred *before* that upgrade. To isolate thin dispatch from planner quality, replay the same dependencies and worker results through competing dispatch mechanisms; an end-to-end quality difference alone cannot attribute the cause to orchestration.
