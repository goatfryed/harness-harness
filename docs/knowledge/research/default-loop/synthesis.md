# Default loop: evidence review

**Date:** 2026-11-23. **Question:** When does a thin orchestrator, strong planner, and mid-tier builder work well? This reviews eight independently written notes on the [starting assumption](../../concepts/default-loop.md). The first round had assigned research angles. The second initially got an excerpt and a support or challenge brief, then received the full concept for re-review without assigned angles. Repeated studies count once; the notes are not eight independent experiments.

## Verdict

**Keep the thin runtime. Treat the model split as a choice, not the default for every nontrivial task.** The combination is a good candidate when the uncertainty is mostly in deciding *what to do*, the resulting work is scoped, and the builder can verify it. If the work is conceptually clear but hard to implement, or if multiple changes share an evolving context, a strong builder or a single strong owner is a better starting candidate. This is an interpretation of the evidence, not a change to the concept.

The full concept already allows solo work for small, clear tasks, local builder judgment, replanning when assumptions break, and a stronger builder after repeated difficulty. The revised notes challenge **initial allocation and escalation timing**, not the absence of those options. In particular: can we identify costly or tightly coupled implementation early enough to start stronger, and can the runtime surface a broken assumption before downstream work goes stale?

| Judgment | What worked or failed | What to take from it |
|---|---|---|
| **Works well: stronger advice at an evidence-rich moment.** | [PAUSE, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.29870v1#S4.T3) raised resolution from **50.2% to 65.0%** by adding one pre-edit consultation to the same SWE-agent executor. [ARIA, §§4–5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13870v1#S5) reached **48.0%** vs. **34.0%** for always-consult under the same four-call cap and nearly the same observed cost (**$22.09 vs. $21.39**). The same small ARIA test scored **62% at $45.90** when the stronger model executed instead. | Buy judgment selectively rather than at every handoff. The hybrid can be an attractive cost–quality point without being the highest-quality option. ARIA still used a model orchestrator; this tests consultation policy, not dispatch. |
| **Works well: executable checks and simple control for known steps.** | In [Agentless v1, §4](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01489v1#S4), deterministic filtering raised resolution from **78 to 82 of 300** issues over the multi-sample voting configuration at the same rounded inference cost. [Cadence, Table 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.12269v1#S5.T4) used code to adjust inspection timing from model verdicts and reached **42.33%** vs. **33.33%** with seven-step inspections at nearly the same realized token spend. | Enforce known checks and limits in code. Let the model interpret ambiguous evidence. This supports individual runtime mechanisms, rather than a measured advantage for deterministic dispatch itself. |
| **Works conditionally: cheaper builders.** | [Cursor's SQLite experiment](https://cursor.com/blog/agent-swarm-model-economics#model-economics) reports **$1,339** for Opus planning with Composer workers vs. **$10,565** for GPT-5.5 in both roles, with similar reported test quality across mixes. But on [ProgramBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.17471v1#S6), assigning cheaper agents to implementation dropped reward below the strong solo baseline; cheaper *investigation* workers helped a strong lead instead. The Cursor price comparison changes both planner and worker models. | Route by the bottleneck, not the role label. For independent, checkable assignments, try a cheaper builder. For difficult implementation, try a strong builder with cheap investigation support rather than paying for increasingly detailed instructions. |
| **Works badly: splitting tightly coupled work without shared judgment.** | In [AsynCodeBench, §5.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.32662v1#S5.SS2), solo beat even the best tested team protocol for **4 of 10 models**; six gained. With a read-only manager, **32 of 190** model–task outcomes passed at least 80% of test points while satisfying fewer than half their dependency checks. On [ParallACT's contradictory specifications](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.00314v1), one Opus-based agent that saw both specs scored **62%** vs. **16%** for split agents. | Give coupled changes a single owner who can see the relevant requirements, or explicitly check producer–consumer behavior after integration. Passing most tests is not the same as closing a dependency. ParallACT changes who sees which information, so it identifies an information-loss risk rather than proving all coordination is harmful. |
| **Works badly: spending a tight budget on planning at the expense of execution.** | In [The Organization of Inference](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20449v1), issue-informed read-only planning reached **45.6%** vs. **55.4%** direct execution in a 12k logical-token panel; an issue-informed planning arm led in a separate 24k panel. In [SWE-EVO, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.18470v6#S4.T2), GPT-5 resolved **10 of 48** release-sized tasks vs. **5 of 48** for GPT-5-mini in the same SWE-agent scaffold. | Preserve execution budget. Bigger models can improve the work itself, not just the plan. The separate 24k campaign does not establish a universal budget threshold, and SWE-EVO compares solo models, not their response to the same external plan. |

## How much weight to give the findings

- **Most decision-relevant:** fixed-executor or same-scaffold comparisons that vary advice, timing, or model assignment. PAUSE, ARIA, Cadence, and the ProgramBench stage ablations address specific decisions. Their tasks and cost measures differ, so the effect sizes should not be pooled.
- **Useful for seeing where the loop breaks:** dependency-focused experiments and strong solo baselines. AsynCodeBench and ParallACT expose concrete failures; neither says every sizable change should be solo. BOAD provides a useful counterexample: manually designed subagents scored **47.4%** against **49.8%** solo, but the searched hierarchy scored **53.2%** on the same SWE-bench Verified setup ([BOAD, results](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23631v1)). How roles are designed matters.
- **Do not count as deployment savings:** the [timeout-cascade simulation](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.24324v1#S5.SS3) reported **16.4%** less estimated GPU time, but selected which cheap patches to keep using benchmark task tests; it also took about twice as long on average at the 120-minute setting. Use checks available to the runtime for escalation; reserve hidden acceptance tests for evaluation.
- **Lower weight for this choice:** function-generation results, noncoding routing and tool-use benchmarks, and engineering retrospectives are useful for generating hypotheses. They do not carry the same weight as fixed-worker repository-repair experiments. The specific three-role mix has not been isolated against strong solo under the same total budget in the reviewed evidence.

## What to test locally

Use the same tasks, repository snapshots, tools, and acceptance checks. Include **ambiguous but separable work**, **clear but hard implementation**, and **coupled work with changing requirements**. Compare:

1. **Strong solo** with the same thin runtime and verification.
2. **The documented default: strong planner + mid-tier builder**, with planning after initial investigation, replanning on material evidence, local builder judgment, and a stronger-builder retry when a clear task repeatedly fails. Include the cost of attempts before and after escalation.
3. **Strong planner + strong builder**, holding the work brief and runtime fixed where feasible to isolate builder capability.
4. **Strong builder + cheap investigation support** to test the opposite allocation.

Compare acceptance under matched dollar and wall-clock caps, and report total spend and elapsed time separately. Count retries, handoffs, integration, and human review. Track incorrect implementation despite a sound plan, stale assignments, failed dependency checks, and missed opportunities to replan. If consultation timing remains in question, vary **when the same planner is called** in a separate comparison instead of changing the builder or scheduler at the same time.
