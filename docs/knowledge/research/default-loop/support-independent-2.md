# Independent evidence: thin orchestration, strong planning, mid-tier building

Source assessed: [Default loop: thin orchestrator, strong planner, mid-tier builder](../../concepts/default-loop.md). Research question: Is its proposed role split, selective planning and escalation policy a defensible starting point for nontrivial software work? Evidence reviewed 23 November 2026. This is a hypothesis about a cost/quality operating point, not proof that this exact three-role arrangement is optimal.

## Evidence

**Direct repository-level evidence — targeted planner.** [PAUSE](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.29870v1), §4.2, Table 3, intercepts the first source edit after the executor has investigated, gives its transcript to a separate stronger planner, and lets the same executor implement and test. On 500 SWE-bench Verified tasks in SWE-agent, Qwen3-Coder resolution rose from 50.2% alone to 65.0% with GPT-5.6 Sol planning. Periodic oversight resolved 67.6% but used $231.71 in planner inference versus $26.13; the difference in resolution was not statistically significant, which does not establish equivalence. Executor budgets, not total dollars, were matched; the paper reports executor tokens separately. This directly supports an evidence-informed checkpoint and local builder ownership, not the particular exception-triggered replan policy or a general claim that the strongest planner always wins.

**Direct but mixed evidence — allocate effort to the bottleneck.** In [Cohere's coding-team study](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.17471v1), §§5–6 and Appendix C.13, the strong lead kept implementation and verification when workers only explored and planned. On 200 ProgramBench reconstruction tasks, that configuration matched the full-stage team’s reward at about half its cost. However, cheaper implementation workers lowered reward below strong solo; on 16 Commit0-Lite tasks, with a supplied repository skeleton, implementation delegation was instead most effective. This lends qualified support to the source's “unclear versus clear but hard” distinction and its advice to consider a stronger builder, while contradicting any blanket preference for cheaper implementers. It does **not** compare stronger-builder escalation directly against replanning after repeated failures, and the paper does not match budgets.

1. **Selective planning can save substantial work.** [AdaCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04220) first generates code and tests it; it introduces an LLM planning agent and rule-based debugging only when needed. Across six open-source LLMs on HumanEval and MBPP, the authors report 27.69% higher average Pass@1, roughly 12× lower token consumption and 16× faster inference than their best baseline, MapCoder. This supports escalating planning when feedback reveals difficulty and using simple test-driven control. **Limits:** function-level benchmarks and comparison against one particularly expensive baseline; not evidence that a strong planner plus a mid-tier executor is best for repository-scale changes.

2. **Orchestration overhead is real and framework-dependent.** An [evaluation of seven agent frameworks across development, vulnerability detection and repair](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00872) finds that AgentOrchestra tends to have longer trajectories and more correction attempts due to coordination overhead. Across the study's three task sets its reported cost was $370.19 versus $16.29 for GPTswarm; framework effectiveness also differed, so this is not a like-for-like efficiency win at equal quality. This is evidence to minimize unnecessary communication and measure end-to-end cost, not a controlled proof that a thin coordinator is superior.

3. **Routing to cheaper models can preserve much of the quality, but it must be calibrated.** [RouteLLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665), an ICLR 2025 study, reports that on MT-Bench a router trained with preference and judge data reached a score of 8.8 versus GPT-4's 9.3 using GPT-4 for 13.4% of requests, with Mixtral 8x7B handling the rest. However, routers trained on Arena data alone performed around random on out-of-distribution MMLU and GSM8K; task-relevant augmentation improved them. This supports testing a cheaper builder and reserving expensive calls for cases where they help, but the study routes whole queries, not planner and builder roles in software engineering; its cost findings depend on then-current model prices.

4. **Verification is a strong candidate for strict enforcement, but process itself can hurt.** In [ClassEval experiments](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09794) on 100 Python class-generation tasks and three models, waterfall-style multi-agent collaboration reduced functional correctness by 37.8% and 39.8% for two models, but increased it by 9.5% for the third; the authors identify testing as the most influential stage and also report new reasoning failures. Tests should be independently executable gates where feasible, rather than an LLM's assertion that work is complete. Passing tests alone does not establish correctness. The paper is a preprint and its results do not establish effects on production repositories.

5. **The exact process changes both cost and output.** A [study of 11 projects, three process models and four models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13942) reports 132 runs, with process and model affecting quality and cost; iterative Agile had better reported quality but higher computational cost than waterfall. The paper's displayed token table and accompanying prose disagree on median values, so its exact token figures should not be relied upon without clarification. It supports a trade-off, not an invariant preference for minimal process.

## Synthesis and practical hypothesis

- **Reasonable default, not established result:** use a narrow controller to decide whether to plan, build, validate, retry or escalate; use an explicitly scoped higher-capability planning pass for complex or ambiguous changes; assign implementation to a capable lower-cost model when the task is well specified. Strong-planner/mid-tier-builder superiority is **not directly isolated** by the sources above.
- **Strict about invariants:** enforce permissions and scope outside model discretion; require observable completion evidence such as relevant tests, exit codes and changed-file inspection; cap retries and escalate repeated failures. These are engineering implications, not measured causal results of the cited papers.
- **Avoid over-planning:** as the source proposes, small clear changes can begin with one capable agent. Revisit planning for broken assumptions, dependencies or scope; when the assignment is clear but repeated implementation fails, compare a stronger builder before another planning round. These trigger choices are plausible hypotheses, not directly isolated by the cited studies. High-risk changes may also require human judgment. A planner can make incorrect assumptions that propagate into all implementation work.
- **Evaluate before standardizing:** compare (A) single strong agent, (B) single mid-tier agent, (C) strong planner + mid-tier builder + thin controller, and (D) C with strong builder. Use the same representative tasks, tools and acceptance tests, randomized task order and repeated runs. Track independently verified completion, regressions, human review time, total token and tool cost, latency, retries and escalation rate; distinguish per-role cost from total end-to-end cost. Include hidden or independently authored tests and audit security-sensitive changes. Report uncertainty and task stratification, not just aggregate pass rate.

**Bottom line:** studies support economical conditional effort, measurable routing, and caution about coordination costs; they do not prove the specified role/model allocation, nor that more planning universally helps. Treat it as a testable default rather than a binding architecture.
