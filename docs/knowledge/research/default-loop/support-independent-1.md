# Independent evidence: thin orchestrator, strong planner, mid-tier builder

Research date: 2026-11-23. Source assessed: [Default loop: thin orchestrator, strong planner, mid-tier builder](../../concepts/default-loop.md). Question: for nontrivial software work, is this a defensible starting loop, with simple next-step orchestration and strict enforcement? Scope: evidence for each component, limitations, and how to test the combined hypothesis. “Thin” describes orchestration logic, not necessarily a small model; “strong” and “mid-tier” are relative to available models on the relevant tasks.

## Bottom line

The assumption is a **plausible, testable default, not an established optimum**. PAUSE offers direct software-repair evidence that a strong planner can help a fixed coding worker after investigation; it does not establish that the complete loop beats a strong solo agent at equal total cost. Evidence on builder tier is mixed: cheaper implementers hurt in one reconstruction study. Cost-routing studies support selectively spending more on harder work, but do not measure this three-role loop. External guardrails are well-supported security practice, distinct from rigidly enforcing every proposed planning step.

## Evidence

1. **Keep coordination simple, adding machinery when it earns its cost.** Anthropic's practitioner guidance recommends simple composable patterns, says orchestrator-workers is suited to complex coding changes with unpredictable subtasks, and advises environmental feedback, sandboxing, and stopping conditions. It also says a single call or simple workflow may suffice, with added agent complexity justified by measured improvement. This is experience-based vendor guidance, not a controlled comparison of thin and complex orchestrators. [Anthropic, *Building effective agents*, 2024](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents).

2. **Planner capability can matter disproportionately.** PEAR compares planner/executor model combinations over 84 AgentDojo-derived banking, Slack, travel and workspace tasks, repeated five times. The authors report weaker planners often reduce utility more than weaker executors, although weak executors also cause substantial declines; e.g., Gemini-2.5-pro planner with Gemini-2.0-flash executor was around 50% utility. Their memory experiments also suggest planner history can be important. This supports *investigating* asymmetric allocation, not assuming that a particular coding builder is capable enough: these are controlled tool-use tasks, not repository repairs; comparisons depend on models, prompts, and iterations. [Dong et al., *PEAR*, §4.2–4.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07505v4#S4).

3. **A concise plan can help software agents, but plans are advisory and can harm.** An analysis of 16,991 SWE-agent trajectories across four models and SWE-bench Verified and Pro reports that a standard navigate/reproduce/patch/validate instruction improved resolution versus no plan on the studied setup; periodic reminders improved adherence and outcomes. It also found some added phases and poor plans hurt, and model behavior varied. This is evidence for planning and feedback, not for a separate planner model or mechanically forcing every phase. A plan's correctness, task fit and ability to change after new evidence matter. [Liu et al., *From Plan to Action*, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.12147v1).

4. **Using cheaper models selectively can improve the cost–quality frontier.** RouteLLM trained routers to select between strong and weak models. On MT-Bench, its augmented matrix-factorization router used strong-model calls for 13.4% of requests to recover 50% of the gap between Mixtral and GPT-4; the resulting score was 8.8 versus GPT-4's 9.3. The paper reports much smaller savings on MMLU and GSM8K, and routing trained only on Arena data performed near random on those out-of-distribution benchmarks. This motivates measurement and escalation, not a promised saving for agentic coding: the study routes individual queries, not planner/builder steps, and uses historical model prices. [Ong et al., *RouteLLM*, §§5.1–5.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18665v4#S5).

5. **Strictly enforce actual boundaries outside model instructions.** OWASP recommends scoped tool permissions, independent tool authorization, sandboxing, bounded retries/cost, and human approval for high-impact actions; approval should bind to the exact action. PEAR also finds attacks directed at the planner and inter-agent communication can compromise downstream actions under its specified attack surfaces, including direct message or system-prompt manipulation. Neither stronger planning nor a trusted-sounding handoff is a security boundary. [OWASP AI Agent Security Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/AI_Agent_Security_Cheat_Sheet.html); [PEAR, §4.6](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07505v4#S4.SS6).

6. **Require evidence of validation, not a ritual.** Tests and tool results supply feedback, but passing tests do not guarantee correct requirements or broad system behavior. One study using mini-SWE-agent on SWE-bench Verified found that prompting four models to write more or fewer new tests changed costs and process more than final outcomes in that setting; this does not show existing tests, carefully chosen regression tests, or verification are useless. Combine relevant automated checks and inspection, report unrun or failing checks, and adapt to the task rather than requiring new tests unconditionally. [Chen et al., *Rethinking the Value of Agent-Generated Tests*, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07900v2); [Anthropic, coding agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents#appendix-1-agents-in-practice).

7. **Plan after gathering evidence; a targeted call can be useful without a permanent lead.** PAUSE intercepts a coding worker's first source edit, gives a separate planner its preceding investigation, and resumes the same worker in the same workspace. With Qwen3-Coder and SWE-agent on 500 SWE-bench Verified issues, a GPT-5.6 Sol planner increased resolution from 50.2% to 65.0%; on the same benchmark periodic oversight reached 67.6% and cost $231.71 in planner inference versus $26.13 for PAUSE. The difference between the two steering methods was not statistically significant; that is not proof of equivalence. Executor budgets, not total inference cost, were matched, and the first-edit hook can miss shell edits. This supports an evidence-rich, limited intervention, not a universal first-edit trigger, an exception-only replan policy, or a win against a frontier solo agent. [Patel et al., *PAUSE*, §§3–5, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.29870v1#S4.SS2).

8. **Allocate effort to the actual bottleneck, and do not assume cheaper implementation is safe.** In 200 ProgramBench reconstruction tasks, Cohere's same-model teams that delegated discovery/planning and retained implementation and verification in a strong lead matched the reported reward of teams delegating throughout at roughly half the cost. Cheaper implementation workers reduced reward below strong solo; a small 16-task Commit0-Lite comparison with an existing repository skeleton instead favored implementation delegation. These results support the source's distinction between uncertain next steps and hard execution, while challenging any blanket mid-tier-builder policy. The studies vary responsibility as well as model tier and do not match costs; “planning” includes black-box behavioral discovery, and the strong lead also implements. They do not directly test swapping builders after repeated failures. [Samway et al., *Scaling Inference-Time Compute with Teams of Coding Agents*, §§5–6 and Appendix C.13](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.17471v1).

## Inference: a minimal candidate loop

For a genuinely nontrivial task, use a bounded coordinator to establish the objective and constraints; investigate enough to inform a concise plan; pass a scoped task and acceptance criteria to a capable but cheaper builder; inspect actual changes and validation output; then finish, revise, or escalate. Replan when evidence invalidates a key assumption, dependencies, or scope; if the assignment remains clear but the builder repeatedly struggles, test a stronger builder before automatically replanning. This failure-routing rule is an inference to test, not a result established by the cited studies. Do not treat a plan as immutable. The coordinator should enforce explicit user requirements, permission and approval rules, task scope, resource caps, and truthful reporting. An exhausted limit is not success. It should not turn model-generated preferences into hard safety rules or make test failure disappear by accepting a self-reported pass.

This is a synthesis of the above evidence, not a configuration directly tested by any one cited study. A single strong agent may win when handoff overhead, coordination errors, or builder limitations dominate; a small edit may need no separate planner at all. Even for complex work, “mid-tier” must be calibrated to the language, repository, tools, and consequence of errors.

## What would distinguish the hypothesis

Run a paired, repeated evaluation on representative real tasks with identical repositories, tools, permissions, time limits and independent hidden acceptance tests or human review. Compare: (A) single strong agent; (B) single mid-tier agent; (C) strong planner + mid-tier builder with thin coordinator; (D) same loop with mid-tier planner; and, where feasible, (E) strong planner + strong builder. Keep prompts and verification as comparable as possible and record versions and task difficulty.

Measure independently adjudicated success and regressions, safety/constraint violations, end-to-end tokens and actual cost including retries, elapsed time, escalation rate, and human intervention. Report paired differences and uncertainty, including task-level failures and tail costs. Test escalation on failed verification, repeated failed attempts, uncertainty or high-risk scope; compare against no escalation. Separately evaluate guardrails with adversarial tool inputs and unauthorized actions. This can falsify the default if the extra handoffs erase savings or reduce success, or if weak planning or building is the dominant bottleneck.

To test the source's specific timing and routing claims, compare the same planning model consulted upfront, after investigation, and upon explicit exceptions, while holding the worker and baseline checks fixed; include direct feedback to the worker without a planner. For repeated failures on a clear assignment, compare a stronger builder against another planning call from the same saved state. Replay continuations and count the total cost of gathering the state, retries, and follow-up; failure classifications themselves need independent review.

## Limitations

Evidence is heterogeneous: vendor experience, general task routing, controlled non-code tool tasks, and software-repair benchmarks do not establish production performance of the complete three-role design. Studies use different models, harnesses, evaluation functions, and periods; future model and price changes may reverse the trade-off. Security advice is normative, while PEAR's attack assumptions include unusually privileged attacker access; neither supplies a universal attack rate. No claim here establishes that strict workflow compliance is inherently desirable or that automated tests substitute for human judgment.
