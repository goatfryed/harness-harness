# Independent challenge: the planner–builder default

**Source assessed:** [Default loop: thin orchestrator, strong planner, mid-tier builder](../../concepts/default-loop.md). **Question:** Is its proposed starting point for nontrivial software work preferable to credible alternatives? This note looks for contrary evidence, not a proof that the arrangement never works. Evidence current to 23 November 2026.

**Scope correction after reading the source:** It explicitly treats the arrangement as a starting assumption, not a settled architecture or fixed model-tier policy; permits a capable solo agent on small, clear tasks; calls the planner after investigation and when material assumptions change, rather than after every handoff; lets the builder make local decisions; and suggests a stronger builder when a clear assignment repeatedly proves difficult. It does not mandate a static plan, universal role handoffs, or strict adherence to every plan phase. The challenge is to the *initial default on nontrivial work*, its allocation rule, and the observability of its escalation triggers—not to those positions the source already rejects.

## Bottom line

The evidence does **not** establish that this specific three-role, asymmetric-model arrangement is a better starting point for *nontrivial* work than a competent single agent with tools and validation, a strong implementing lead with cheaper investigators, or other adaptive configurations. Handoffs and incorrect plans can consume budget or degrade outcomes, and the first assignment may matter before repeated failure triggers escalation. A plausible alternative is to select the initial workflow using task characteristics and observed evidence, rather than nontriviality alone. That is a hypothesis to test locally, not a universal replacement rule.

## Evidence against the assumptions

1. **More roles are not automatically better for real repository fixes.** In [Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296), a controlled comparison across multiple architectures reports mean SWE-bench Verified resolution of 0.522 for single agents versus 0.511 hybrid, 0.506 centralized, 0.494 decentralized, and 0.444 independent multi-agent setups. SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench used only 20 tasks per configuration, so these small differences should not be overinterpreted or generalized to every role design. Nevertheless, they directly challenge a presumption that coordination pays for itself on ordinary issue resolution.

2. **Manually assigned roles can be worse than no delegation.** [BOAD](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23631) reports 49.8% Verified resolution for its Seed-OSS-36B single-agent baseline, 47.4% with manually designed subagents, and 53.2% with a searched hierarchy. The result is not anti-hierarchy: the learned hierarchy won in that setting, and its total tokens were approximately equal on Verified and lower on Live. Rather, it challenges treating a particular hand-designed planner/builder team as intrinsically correct. Comparisons are model- and scaffold-specific; hierarchy search also incurs development-time cost.

3. **Planning quality and execution alignment matter more than assigning a strong planner.** [Evaluating Plan Compliance in Autonomous Programming Agents](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.12147) examines 21,120 SWE-agent trajectories with four models and plan variations. Its standard plan improved aggregate resolution over no plan, but a deficient plan could hurt more than none, added early phases sometimes degraded performance, and behavior differed by model. Some no-plan-only successes involved unsuccessful attempts to create a reproduction test. This is not evidence to omit validation or reproduction; it is evidence against requiring the same detailed plan for every task and against equating compliance with success. Its harder SWE-bench Pro analysis used a selected subset of 31 solvable Python instances, limiting generalization.

4. **A plan handoff can fail even if the planner is capable.** [Do LLM Agents Execute the Plans They Declare?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38108) reports generic Plan+ReAct preserved declared structure in only about 22–45% of trajectories across three benchmarks. Its pattern-specific executors improved SWE-bench Verified success from 0.36 to 0.442 in one model comparison; model choice of planning mode remained unreliable. Structural fidelity is not itself proof of correctness, and this study does not isolate planner strength or mid-tier builders. It does suggest that a detailed static handoff is not a reliable substitute for feedback, observable state, and replanning.

5. **Spend can be more valuable on alternatives than on a role hierarchy.** [HAI-Agent](https://proceedings.colm.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/file/f77b8a0f769a62fa2d68ddbc884afabb-Paper-Conference.pdf) reports, on SWE-bench Pro with Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, 43.2% resolution for one SWE-agent, 39.9% for naive multi-agent, 47.2% for governed multi-agent, and 49.6% for best-of-five single-agent trajectories with an independent LLM selector. Both latter approaches used approximately five times the baseline compute; the selector did not see ground-truth tests. The authors report stronger relative results for governed handoffs on longer-horizon Commit0-lite, so this is not a general verdict against multi-agent systems. It does illustrate why compare at matched end-to-end cost and distinguish deployable selection from an oracle.

6. **Initial builder allocation is not settled by a later escalation path.** [Scaling Inference-Time Compute with Teams of Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.17471v1) found that, on 200 black-box ProgramBench reconstruction tasks, a strong lead using cheap workers for discovery/planning and implementing itself outperformed its strong-solo baseline, while cheap implementation workers reduced reward below strong solo and increased cost. “Planning” includes probing a reference executable, not just strategic decisions, and the strong lead both plans and codes; budgets were not matched. On 16 Commit0-Lite tasks, implementation delegation instead worked best. This is a direct counterexample to assuming cheaper implementation is always the best *initial* allocation, not to the source's explicit stronger-builder escalation. Early retries can already have incurred cost, context loss, or poor design lock-in; those consequences are hypotheses to measure, not results isolated by this study.

7. **Adaptive allocation may cross role boundaries.** [TwinRouterBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18859) reports a trained step-level router resolving 75/100 cases for $25.66 in API spend versus 74/100 for always-Opus at $54.73, while rule-based routing performed worse on its static trajectory-preservation measure. The dynamic set was a nonrandom, two-repository slice, and results do not isolate coding versus planning steps. This is compatible with the source's non-fixed policy, but suggests per-step allocation as another comparator; cheap routing can silently break a whole trajectory.

## Where this challenges the actual source

| Source claim | Specific unresolved challenge |
|---|---|
| Start with the three responsibilities on nontrivial work | SWE-bench architectural comparisons and BOAD's manual-role baseline motivate testing solo work even for bounded, nontrivial issues. The source's solo exception is narrower: small, clear tasks. Sample size and scaffold differences prevent a universal conclusion. |
| More planning when unclear; more building when clear but hard | ProgramBench suggests inexpensive parallel discovery plus strong implementation can be valuable even when the task is unclear; uncertainty and implementation difficulty can coexist. The rule is a useful heuristic, not an experimentally isolated allocation law. |
| Bring the planner back when assumptions break, dependencies change, or scope shifts | These triggers require someone or something to notice the break. Plan-compliance and handoff evidence suggest measuring missed signals and stale assignments; they do not prove that periodic replanning is superior. |
| Try a stronger builder after repeated difficulty with a clear assignment | Compare proactive strong implementation on costly or hard-to-reverse tasks with retry-then-escalate. Existing cited experiments do not establish the best escalation threshold. |
| Thin dispatch and strict checks | Neither multi-agent success nor planning research proves the minimum monitoring or authority needed for reliable enforcement. The source already asks the orchestrator to track results and route failures; this note does not assume it blindly trusts self-reports. |

**Evidence supporting the source also matters.** Its [supporting research](../thin-orchestration-smart-planners.md) reports PAUSE's fixed-worker improvement after an investigation-stage planning intervention (50.2% to 65.0% on SWE-agent), and DCAS reports a stronger external plan outperforming self-planning with the same executor (57.8% versus 48.2%). Neither comparison matches total cost to a capable solo frontier model. Its [adaptive-lead review](../adaptive-model-leads.md) also finds selective steering can help without showing a persistent lead always pays off. These findings support testing the source's design; they do not establish it as the best initial policy across nontrivial work.

## Implications for “strict enforcement”

Distinguish **invariants** from **workflow prescription**. Permission boundaries, destructive-action approval, protected data, and honest reporting should be enforced regardless of agent architecture. Tests, acceptance criteria, and review should be evidence-based gates appropriate to risk, with explicit reporting when unavailable. Mandating every plan phase or adherence to a stale plan can be counterproductive, but the source does not require either. The plan-compliance paper supports this distinction, not relaxation of security controls.

A thin orchestrator also needs enough visibility to detect handoff failure, conflicting changes, missing tests, or an invalid plan. “Simple” should mean few decision rules, not blind acceptance of a builder’s self-report. When work is genuinely decomposable or exceeds one context, governed multi-agent handoffs may be appropriate; the HAI-Agent and BOAD results are important counterevidence to an unconditional single-agent default.

## Falsifiable local comparison

On a representative, held-out set of actual repository tasks, compare (A) one capable agent with tools and verification; (B) the proposed strong-planner/mid-tier-builder/orchestrator; and (C) one capable agent with retries or independent review, under comparable total cost and wall-clock limits. Predefine safety invariants, acceptance tests, task difficulty and decomposition categories. Record resolved rate, regressions, human review findings, end-to-end spend including planning and retries, latency, handoff failures, and escalation frequency. Use repeated runs and paired task-level uncertainty estimates. Include long-horizon and parallelizable work, not only small bug fixes; do not use inaccessible ground-truth tests for candidate selection. If B wins only a subset, route to it on measurable task characteristics rather than making it universal.

**Limitations.** These studies vary models, prompts, tools, compute budgets, and definitions of roles; none directly establishes the optimal strong-planner/mid-tier-builder split for this environment. Benchmark tests imperfectly capture security, maintainability, product judgment, and human collaboration. Cost figures and model rankings age quickly; local experiments must use current prices and models.
