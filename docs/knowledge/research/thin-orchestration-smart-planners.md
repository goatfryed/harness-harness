# Dumb orchestrator, smart planners

**Evidence reviewed through:** 2026-11-23. **Scope:** A focused follow-up to [workflow patterns](workflow-patterns.md) and the [research goal](goal.md), not a harness design decision.

## Bottom line

**A dumb runtime is not a dumb decision-maker.** Recent experiments show that a model can author a useful workflow and let code run it, and that the same coding worker can fare differently depending on who makes the next decision. The evidence supports keeping routine dispatch and enforcement separate from judgment about what to do next. It does **not** establish that a dedicated planner is always worth a handoff, that the strongest model belongs in planning rather than implementation, or that upfront planning can replace feedback.

- **Moderate evidence that workflow authorship matters.** WorkflowArena holds the worker model fixed and finds meaningful gains from one workflow author, but mixed or negative results from another strong model. An executable workflow can transfer useful judgment; model strength on the end task is not a reliable proxy for that ability [1].
- **Direct evidence that a single well-timed planning call can help a smaller coding agent.** PAUSE improved repair resolution across three harnesses by asking a stronger model for advice after investigation but before the first edit. DCAS separately found that the choice of planner mattered even when the executor stayed fixed. Neither demonstrates a win over a frontier solo agent at equal total cost [8, 9].
- **Moderate evidence that the best place to spend model effort depends on the bottleneck.** Cursor's SQLite swarm made effective use of cheaper implementers; on ProgramBench, Cohere found cheaper workers helpful for investigation but harmful when assigned implementation. The studies put different responsibilities under “planner” [3, 4].
- **Limited evidence that eliminating a persistent model lead improves end-to-end coding.** There are viable examples and partial comparisons, but no result in this review holds the planner, worker, verification, and budget fixed while varying only whether a model or a runtime owns routine dispatch across representative coding tasks.

## What “dumb” and “smart” mean here

| Responsibility | What a thin runtime can enforce | What still needs judgment |
|---|---|---|
| What runs next | Dispatch eligible work after declared prerequisites; route results to the owner; stop or pause at limits. | Decide which work is worth doing, which dependencies are real, and when to revise the plan. |
| What to do when work fails | Distinguish failed, blocked, canceled, and exhausted work; retry known transient errors within a cap. | Diagnose a substantive failure; choose between local correction, broader replanning, or escalation. |
| What counts as done | Run designated checks, record evidence, and refuse success when a required gate is unmet. | Decide whether checks cover the user's intent and resolve ambiguous or conflicting evidence. Where appropriate, ask the user. |

This table is an **interpretation**, not a measured role taxonomy. A planner that owns decomposition, cross-task decisions, and replanning is doing semantic orchestration even if it never dispatches a job or edits code. “Thin” describes where judgment lives, not how much engineering the runtime takes. A planner-authored script can also branch on results without a fresh planning call; actual worker outputs are still stochastic.

## What the evidence isolates

| Source | Comparison and result | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| **PAUSE**, October 2026 [8] | A hook intercepts the first detected source edit, sends the investigation transcript to a tool-free GPT-5.6 Sol planner, and resumes the same Qwen3-Coder executor in its existing context and workspace. On SWE-bench Verified, resolution rose **50.2% → 65.0%** in SWE-agent, **45.4% → 62.2%** in OpenHands, and **37.0% → 43.0%** in Pi. In SWE-agent, periodic oversight reached 67.6% but cost about **9×** as much in planner inference; the score difference was not statistically significant. | Matched executor budgets are not matched total inference cost. This is one intervention with an autonomous worker, not a general multi-worker scheduler. The scores do not prove equivalence to periodic oversight, or that first-edit timing beats giving the same planner an upfront turn. The hook can miss edits through the shell. |
| **DCAS**, October 2026 [9] | With the same Qwen3-Coder-30B executor and Claude Code scaffold, SWE-bench Verified Pass@1 rose from **42.8%** without an explicit plan to **48.2%** with a self-generated plan and **57.8%** with a Sonnet 4.5 plan. Opus 4.5 plans scored **54.2–56.0%**, depending on routing. | Execution was capped at 100 turns in all arms, but both planning arms allowed up to 500 extra read-only exploration turns. Comparing self-planning to external planning controls the planning allowance, not realized compute or what was discovered. This does not test a thin dispatch runtime. |
| **WorkflowArena**, November 2026 [1] | On 32 selected hard tasks drawn from a cross-domain benchmark, the authors held each execution model fixed and varied who authored its workflow. The source model could not edit the workspace, and authoring counted against the same wall-clock budget. Opus 5.5 workflows gained **6.41 points static / 6.27 dynamic** in mean continuous score over executors' own dynamic workflows, averaged across four executors; Astra workflows gained **−2.53 / +1.34**. “Static” means the source stops after authoring; its program can still branch and loop. | The two Opus averages do not establish equivalence or show that feedback is unnecessary: individual executors favored different modes. Selected mixed-domain tasks, not a coding-only effect or dollar-matched comparison. This isolates workflow source better than most end-to-end demos, but the workflow includes worker instructions, not just dispatch topology. |
| **LoopArena v2**, October 2026 [2] | On 80 full coding tasks, three repeats each, the Qwen3.7-Plus worker stayed fixed. The best adaptive controller reached **36.25% strict success at $34.68/run**, vs. **31.25% at $9.47** for fixed control and **27.50% at $2.61** with no control. Some model controllers fell below fixed control. | Fixed control only restates the goal; it is not a structured workflow with event-driven replanning. Costs are no-cache estimates, not equal realized spend. This tests the value and cost of deciding the next assignment from evidence, not whether the runtime needs a model to deliver it. |
| **Cursor swarm**, February and July 2026 [3] | Cursor reports abandoning a rigid upfront plan and then an overloaded continuous executor in favor of recursive scope-owning planners that receive worker handoffs and can replan. In a later SQLite reconstruction, the new harness beat the old across four model mixes at the four-hour mark: **73–85% vs. 11–77%** on a held-out SQL suite; one old run had been paused early. The vendor reports **$1,339** for Opus 4.8 planning with Composer 2.5 workers vs. **$10,565** for GPT-5.5 in both roles. | One vendor-reported build. Both planner and worker models change in the price comparison; no full model-role matrix or formally scored solo quality baseline. Harness changes include version control, design reconciliation, and review. The cost figures are not the isolated effect of thinner orchestration or cheaper workers. |
| **Cohere coding teams**, October 2026 [4] | On 200 ProgramBench reconstruction tasks, same-model teams that delegated only discovery/planning matched the reported reward of delegation across planning, implementation, and verification at about **half the cost**. The strong lead still decided what to build, wrote code, and verified it. Cheaper discovery/planning workers recovered **47.5% of the all-strong team's reward gain over solo for 13.9% of its added cost**; cheaper implementers cut reward below strong solo. On the small Commit0-Lite comparison, where a repository skeleton already supplied structure, implementation delegation did best instead. | No equal-cost ablation. “Planning” includes learning a black-box program's behavior, not just choosing a design. This supports spending extra compute where it buys information or progress, not replacing the lead with a thin dispatcher. |
| **Planning-as-Routing**, September 2026 [5] | A deterministic router maps a model-declared mode to a matching executor. On SWE-bench Verified, the authors report **36.0% → 44.2%** patch success for DeepSeek V4 Flash vs. passing a declared plan to generic ReAct. Their declaration policies trailed the best fixed pattern for each tested benchmark–model pair. | The router and pattern-specific executors are a package; some executors themselves use model calls to coordinate. This does not isolate routing, and preserving the prescribed execution structure does not prove the plan was good or that deviations were mistakes. |

Three implementation examples clarify the distinction:

- **LLMCompiler** makes the split explicit: a model emits a dependency graph, a non-model scheduler dispatches ready tool calls, and the model can replan after observing results. Its HotpotQA comparison questions ran **1.8× faster** than ReAct with slightly lower accuracy (**62.00% vs. 62.47%**). That comparison bundles planning, parallelism, and prompt changes; it is adjacent tool-use evidence, not a coding result or a dispatcher-only ablation [10].
- **Claude Code dynamic workflows** let a model write a script that owns loops, branches, and intermediate results while the runtime handles execution and tracks agent results. The documentation establishes the mechanism, not its efficacy [6].
- **Anthropic's long-running app harness** starts with a high-level product spec and leaves implementation decisions to the builder. The author dropped mandatory sprint decomposition when switching to Opus 4.6, but retained planning and targeted evaluation where useful. The earlier game-maker comparison cost **$200 and six hours** for the full harness vs. **$9 and 20 minutes** solo; the expanded product scope and unequal budgets prevent attributing the difference to a separate planner [7].

## When the hypothesis is most worth testing

- **Bounded task, clear outcome:** Use a capable solo coding loop as the baseline. Compare self-planning and, if the agent tends to commit to the wrong fix, one evidence-driven planning call before editing. Test these separately from concurrency: the [earlier review](workflow-patterns.md#findings-and-evidence-strength) found that native concurrency often added cost and was not a reliable win for bounded work.
- **Ambiguous objective or uncertain requirements:** Compare read-only investigation or alternative designs with a dedicated scope owner that can revise the plan on evidence. A wrong plan executed faithfully is still wrong. Track whether failure came from poor task selection, a lost handoff, or poor execution.
- **Well-partitioned implementation:** Test whether cheaper workers can make independent, verifiable changes. For tightly coupled work, compare against strong single-owner implementation: negotiation over shared design decisions and integration can erase the savings from cheaper workers. Cursor's contradictory design decisions and merge contention are concrete failure modes [3].
- **Failures or surprises:** Compare a model lead making decisions at each meaningful boundary with an executable workflow that calls for replanning only when evidence invalidates the plan. Distinguish a flaky check from a violated assumption and an exhausted budget from completion. Measure stale results, needless replans, and premature “done,” not just the final score.

## The comparison that would reduce uncertainty here

For the same bounded fixes, well-partitioned migrations, and evolving cross-module changes, compare **(A)** a capable solo agent, **(B)** a planner-authored executable workflow with the planner returning at explicit exceptions, and **(C)** a model lead making the next decision at each task boundary. Keep initial instructions, worker model, tools, acceptance tests, and resource caps fixed where the comparison permits. In B and C, start from the same initial plan and use the same planning model to test *when it is consulted* rather than *who wrote the better initial plan*. Add a fixed-workflow reference if the procedure is already known; swap planner and worker model tiers in a separate experiment.

Report held-out acceptance, total spend (including reports, retries, and integration), wall time, human interventions, wrong or stale assignments, and off-scope changes. Compare equal-budget outcomes as well as cost at a common quality target. Log which evidence triggered replanning and whether it improved acceptance. To isolate routine dispatch specifically, replay the same dependency plans and worker-result traces through both dispatchers under the same limits; compare readiness violations, scheduling decisions, and overhead separately from end-to-end quality. These comparisons would test whether the Objective Driver and Tasker hypotheses in [goal.md](goal.md) need different workflows, rather than assuming the role names imply different runtimes.
